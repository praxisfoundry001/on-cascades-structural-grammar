# On Cascades: A Structural Grammar

## Subtitle

Pressure, Motion, and the Mistaking of Momentum for Authority

## What is this?

This is a method / structural grammar artifact about cascades: sequences of interaction that begin moving in one direction because of momentum, not because of a decision.

It explains how interaction under pressure becomes directional, why cascade motion can feel authoritative, and why motion should not be mistaken for authority.

## Why should I care?

Cascades happen in classrooms, meetings, families, institutions, emergencies, public controversies, professional settings, and ordinary conversations. They often feel urgent before they are justified.

This artifact gives a plain-language grammar for recognizing cascades early, before motion hardens into boundary, ratchet, policy, record, discipline, or institutional commitment.

## What is the claim?

The central claim is:

A cascade is when things start moving in one direction because of momentum, not because of a decision.

Cascades are motion under pressure. They can warn, reveal pressure, expose fragility, and show where a system is narrowing. But they cannot authorize themselves.

## What is this not?

This artifact does not explain brains, define consciousness or emotion, adjudicate responsibility, replace neuroscience, psychology, institutional analysis, professional judgment, law, or ethics, prescribe one universal intervention, or introduce new canon-level failure regimes.

The five geometries named here are local cascade-level failure geometries, not replacements for the canon-level Structural Flow failure regimes.

## How was this done?

The artifact uses the Structural Flow hinge grammar to clarify Cascade as a distinct structural location before boundary, ratchet, and authority. It translates the idea into human-facing prose through ordinary examples and five local cascade-level failure geometries: dilution, oversaturation, shear, sequestration, and interference.
